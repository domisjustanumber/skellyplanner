Coverage rule

Person height runs from the far end of the usable depth to the near end. In RTMW's training data (AI Challenger and COCO) most people were 85–880 px tall, and a quarter were under 256 px. RTMW crops the person before it looks, so only the height in pixels matters to it. YOLO-pose looks at the whole frame shrunk to 640 px, so it only gets the last figure.

Coverage by camera count

For each number of cameras, the best layout found for that number on the plan's lens: the share of the volume covered; the share where a camera at the subject's side or behind them also sees their back; the share where two such cameras see it, which is what tracks hands behind the back; and the share that stays covered when any one view is blocked. One blocked view hides the subject from every camera inside a 30° slice of directions, which is about what a second person hides standing a metre away; it also stands for a camera that fails, drops frames or misses the subject. How the room planner decides. A camera sees a floor spot when the spot is inside its horizontal field of view, far enough away that a full body fits top to bottom, and close enough that the person is at least as tall as the model crop. A spot is covered when each side of the subject's body is seen by two cameras whose angle apart, measured at that spot, sits inside the pair-angle band, and at least one camera is within 45° of the way the subject faces (single-camera error is flat out to about 45° and rises past it); without that the spot is not covered. A camera looking square at the subject's other side (60° to 120° round from their front) does not count for the side it cannot see, and all of this has to hold for every direction in the turning arc. Cameras stand on the walls, every half metre or in a corner, at least 0.4 m from each other, and aim at the volume's centre or the middle of one of its edges. The plan for the number of cameras you have is made for that number alone. The first camera is face-on to the subject: dead ahead of them, seen from the middle of the volume, on the front wall. That is the view pose models are trained on most and read best. If turning the subject 45° to face a front corner covers the volume with fewer cameras, the plan does that and the face-on camera stands in the corner. Only when no number of cameras covers the volume either way do two cameras stand either side of straight ahead instead. With a turning arc, face-on means face-on to the middle of the arc. Each further camera goes where it does the most for the first thing still unfinished: covering the volume, then seeing the back at every spot, then seeing it with two cameras (hands behind the back), then keeping the most covered with one view blocked. Cameras that do the same are split by the training views they stand for: the views within 15° of the camera that no earlier camera is within 15° of, counted by how common they are in training and how well they measured. That keeps the second camera as near the front as still makes a pair with the first, and puts a rear view straight behind the subject when it has the choice. The third camera is a rear view, at least 90° round, placed where it sees the back in the most of the covered volume: one rear view does more for hands behind the back than a third camera in front. When the subject faces the front wall from the middle of the room's width, or a corner along the diagonal of a square room, a camera to one side is followed by its mirror image, so both sides of the body get the same views. This is done with the volume in each position it can take, and the best result for your number of cameras is used, so the layout for five cameras need not contain the layout for four. The planner also builds layouts the older way, starting from the same first camera and adding cameras (as mirrored pairs where the room allows) until the volume is covered, then until the back is seen everywhere, then until hands behind it are tracked, and uses one of those when it does better. None of this is an exact optimum. Type a distance in "Space in front of volume" to place the volume yourself. With Auto, all of this is done for every stock lens in both landscape and portrait, and the lens that does most with the cameras you have is used: first a lens whose layout has a camera face-on to the subject, then the one with the highest score: the share covered, plus half the share with the back seen, plus a quarter of the share with hands behind the back tracked. So 1 point more covered is worth 2 points more back seen: a few points of cover do not outweigh seeing the back in much more of the volume, and since the back only counts where the volume is covered, a lens cannot win on the back alone. Ties go to the most covered, then back seen, hands and covered with one view blocked, then the fewest cameras that cover it, then the longer lens (more pixels on the person), then landscape. So the lens can change with the number of cameras. With a fixed lens the same choice is made between its two orientations. The verdict says which sensor is better the same way, with the cameras you have.

Why one lens per sensor works. With a fixed lens, the near limit (full body fits) and far limit (enough pixels) both scale with focal length, so their ratio is set by the sensor and crop alone. Landscape at a 256 crop gives about 3.8× for the AR0234 and 2.6× for the OV9281. Changing the lens only slides that band nearer or farther, which is why the best lens depends on how far back your room lets cameras stand. The far limit is the same for both sensors with the same lens; the AR0234 lets people come about a third closer.

Where the rule comes from. Measured with RTMW on CMU Panoptic recordings of single adults 2 to 4 m from the cameras, against the dataset's 3D joints. Triangulating from two cameras, joints were about 37 mm off when the cameras were 60° to 120° apart, 40 mm at 45° to 60°, 46 mm at 30° to 45° and at 120° to 135°, and 70 mm or worse past 135°. From one camera, joints on the side of the body facing it stayed within 15 to 18 mm all the way round to side-on, while joints on the far side went from 19 mm (front view) to about 50 mm (side view) and back to about 30 mm (from behind). Pairs with both cameras within 60° of the subject's front did best (27 to 28 mm); pairs behind the subject were close (32 to 41 mm), with a rare left-right mix-up. Half the joints were within 30 mm both at 30° to 45° apart and at 45° to 60°, and pairs in front of the subject 30° to 45° apart averaged 37 mm, the same as 60° to 120° pairs overall, so the pair-angle band starts at 30°. Where the cameras should look from comes from RTMW's training images (AI Challenger and COCO, 401,394 people): 39% are seen from within 15° of straight ahead, 58% within 30°, 70% within 45° and 13% from behind. Accuracy follows that in front and at the side, but not behind: views from behind are rare in training and still measured as well as the front, so the planner counts them in full. Samani et al. (BMC Research Notes, 2026) found the same shape with MediaPipe: about 45° apart good, about 135° poor. Walker et al. (J. Biomech., 2026, Theia3D) saw tracking fail where camera views didn't cover the volume.

What that measurement does not cover. One pose model, one camera dome, people at least 300 px tall, two cameras at a time. The "each side of the body" rule is a reading of the single-camera result; it was not tested directly. Cameras higher than about 50° above the subject cost about 13 mm; below that, height barely mattered for a subject in clear view.

Hands behind the back. In the same recordings, with the torso modelled as a box between the shoulders and hips, a wrist held behind the back was in clear view of 91 to 100% of cameras 90° or more round from the subject's front, 44% at 75° to 90°, and 8 to 21% nearer the front (67 person-frames, about 1,400 views). Cameras that can't see a wrist still guess where it is, usually near the hip, so two front cameras put a wrist behind the back about 105 mm off (median), within 50 mm only 38% of the time. Adding one clear rear view helps only partly: 24 to 64 mm median depending on the software, 44 to 69% within 50 mm, because one view gives a direction, not a depth. Two clear views 45° to 120° apart at the subject's side or behind them bring it to 11 to 15 mm median and 89 to 98% within 50 mm, provided the software throws out the views that disagree (and does best when it also drops views where the model's wrist score is low); averaging every view gives 40 mm. That is why the plan buys one rear camera and then a second one there: the first sees the back, the second tracks hands behind it. Over every pose in these recordings, wrists were hidden by the torso from 9% of front views and about 36 to 40% of views from behind, so the front cameras still matter most for hands in general.

Blocked views. In the same recordings a second person in front of the subject cost far more than any camera angle: about 56 mm in that camera's view. Triangulating three standing people with one such view among the cameras, joints were 106 mm off from two cameras (51 mm with both views clear). From three cameras they were 45 mm off, and from four 34 mm, when the software dropped the view that disagreed with the others (38 and 31 mm with every view clear), but 72 and 69 mm when it averaged all views. So a blocked view stops mattering only when the views that are left still cover the spot and the software throws the bad one out, and that is what "covered with one view blocked" counts: the spot must still pass the whole rule, straight-on camera included, with every camera inside any one 30° slice of directions gone. The 30° is a judgement (a person half a metre wide, a metre from the subject), and so is treating a missed detection, a dropped frame or a dead camera as the same thing. With the fewest cameras almost no spot passes; most of the volume usually passes with two to three times as many, and with a wide turning arc 20 may not be enough. The planner does not model furniture, a subject on the floor, or where a second person actually stands. Camera height is not modelled either, and it probably helps: with three people standing together, views from 15° to 45° above the subject were about half as far off as views from near eye level (36 to 39 mm against 66 to 70 mm in the image), and from above 45° someone nearer overlapped the subject in 6% of views against 30 to 43% lower down. That comparison was not controlled for anything else, so take it as a reason to mount cameras high rather than as a rule.

The map is a floor plan: it ignores camera height and assumes one subject. Square volumes are used for the lens sweep. Fields of view assume a rectilinear lens; cheap wide M12 lenses have barrel distortion, so real coverage is a little wider with fewer pixels near the frame edge. Both sensors have 3.0 µm pixels. Depth of field uses a circle of confusion of two pixels.
